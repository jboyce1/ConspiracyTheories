    M2.1a · Conspiracy Theories

    Decision Making

    In this lesson we cover how humans are bad at seeing the truth for what it is, our natural tendency is to take mental shortcuts. Where that often serves us well we should be mindful of when and how we are doing it. 

    Overarching Question:

    What are the limitations we have when deciding whether to believe in conspiracies?

    Objectives:   

    M1.2 Class 1

    Standards:   

    Stage 1: Identification – Meta-Analysis of Decision Making

    Goal: Identify the decision of choosing a capstone project for the course and reflect on its impact for the semester.

    • You are selecting a capstone project that will be the focus of your work throughout the entire semester. This decision will shape not only what you study, but how you approach research, critical thinking, and the development of your final presentation.

      • Question 1: What do you think makes a strong capstone project? Consider factors like your interest, the availability of research, and the complexity of the topic.

      • Question 2: How do you want this project to challenge you? (Think about skills you want to develop: research, analysis, writing, presenting, critical thinking.)

      • Question 3: How does choosing this project now affect the direction of your work for the semester? (Think about the long-term impact of your choice. What kind of project will keep you engaged? What type of project will allow you to explore the most interesting questions?)

    Example Response: "For my capstone project, I need to choose a conspiracy theory that is both interesting and complex enough to explore over the entire semester. I want to challenge myself by selecting a topic with multiple competing explanations and a wide range of sources to analyze. My decision now will affect the kinds of research I do, the critical thinking skills I develop, and the depth of my final presentation."

    Rubric for Stage 1: Identification – Meta-Analysis of Decision Making

    Criterion 1: Understanding of What Makes a Strong Capstone Project

    Meets Expectations

    • Content: The student identifies at least three specific factors that contribute to a strong capstone project (e.g., personal interest, availability of research materials, complexity of the topic).

    • Explanation: Provides a clear and detailed explanation for each factor, showing thoughtful consideration.

    • Relevance: Factors are directly relevant to the successful completion of a semester-long capstone project.

    Approaches Meeting Expectations

    • Content: The student identifies two factors that contribute to a strong capstone project.

    • Explanation: Provides some explanation for each factor but lacks depth or detail.

    • Relevance: Factors are generally relevant but may be somewhat vague or not fully applicable to a capstone project.

    Fails to Meet Expectations

    • Content: The student identifies one or no factors, or the factors listed are irrelevant.

    • Explanation: Provides little to no explanation, or explanations are unclear or off-topic.

    • Relevance: Factors are not relevant to a capstone project or show misunderstanding of the assignment.

    Criterion 2: Reflection on Personal Challenges and Skill Development

    Meets Expectations

    • Content: The student specifies at least two specific skills they want the project to help them develop (e.g., research, analysis, writing, presenting, critical thinking).

    • Explanation: Clearly articulates how they want the project to challenge them in developing these skills.

    • Self-Awareness: Demonstrates self-awareness by connecting the desired skills to personal growth goals.

    Approaches Meeting Expectations

    • Content: The student specifies one skill they want to develop.

    • Explanation: Provides a basic explanation of how the project might help develop this skill but lacks detail.

    • Self-Awareness: Shows limited connection between the skill and personal growth goals.

    Fails to Meet Expectations

    • Content: The student does not specify any skills they wish to develop.

    • Explanation: Provides no explanation, or the response is off-topic or unrelated to skill development.

    • Self-Awareness: Does not demonstrate self-awareness or consideration of personal growth.

    Criterion 3: Consideration of Long-Term Impact on Semester Work

    Meets Expectations

    • Content: The student clearly explains how their project choice will affect their work throughout the semester.

    • Depth of Analysis: Discusses at least two specific long-term impacts, such as sustained engagement, depth of research, or ability to explore interesting questions.

    • Forward-Thinking: Shows insight into how early decisions shape future outcomes.

    Approaches Meeting Expectations

    • Content: The student mentions how their project choice might affect their semester but in general terms.

    • Depth of Analysis: Discusses one long-term impact but lacks detail or depth.

    • Forward-Thinking: Shows some consideration of future implications but may be superficial.

    Fails to Meet Expectations

    • Content: The student does not discuss the long-term impact of their project choice.

    • Depth of Analysis: Provides no analysis, or the response is irrelevant.

    • Forward-Thinking: Does not demonstrate forward-thinking or understanding of how the project choice affects future work.

    Criterion 4: Clarity, Precision, and Conciseness of Response

    Meets Expectations

    • Clarity: The response is easy to understand and free of ambiguity.

    • Precision: Answers are specific and directly address each question.

    • Conciseness: Information is presented succinctly without unnecessary filler.

    • Organization: The response is well-organized, with each question answered separately and logically.

    Approaches Meeting Expectations

    • Clarity: The response is generally understandable but may contain some vague statements.

    • Precision: Answers address the questions but may include irrelevant details.

    • Conciseness: Response is somewhat wordy or includes unnecessary repetition.

    • Organization: The response lacks clear organization or blends answers together.

    Fails to Meet Expectations

    • Clarity: The response is difficult to understand or contains ambiguous statements.

    • Precision: Does not directly answer the questions; responses are off-topic.

    • Conciseness: The response is overly verbose with significant filler content.

    • Organization: The response is disorganized and confusing.

    Scoring Guidelines

    • To "Meet Expectations" for the assignment, the student must achieve "Meets Expectations" in all four criteria.
    • To "Approach Meeting Expectations," the student achieves "Approaches Meeting Expectations" in at least two criteria and no "Fails to Meet Expectations" in any criterion.
    • "Fails to Meet Expectations" applies if the student receives "Fails to Meet Expectations" in one or more criteria.

    Example of a "Meets Expectations" Response

    Question 1: What do you think makes a strong capstone project?

    "A strong capstone project is built on a topic that deeply interests me, ensuring sustained engagement throughout the semester. It should have ample research materials available from credible sources so I can build a well-supported analysis. Additionally, the topic should be complex enough to allow for critical thinking and exploration of multiple perspectives."

    Question 2: How do you want this project to challenge you?

    "I want this project to challenge me by enhancing my research skills, particularly in evaluating the credibility of sources. I also aim to improve my critical thinking by analyzing conflicting information and forming my own evidence-based conclusions."

    Question 3: How does choosing this project now affect the direction of your work for the semester?

    "Choosing a compelling topic now will set the foundation for my semester. It will keep me motivated to dive deep into research and encourage me to ask meaningful questions. This early decision ensures that my work remains focused and allows me to manage my time effectively for a thorough investigation."

    Stage 2: Consideration and Discretion – Selecting a Conspiracy Theory and Group Work Decision

    Goal: Encourage students to explore a wide range of conspiracy theories, weigh the pros and cons of each, and consider whether working individually or in a group will be more effective for their project. Students will also develop some of their own criteria for selection in addition to those provided.

    • Brainstorming:
      • Write down at least five conspiracy theories that you are considering. For each theory, briefly summarize the "official story" and the "conspiracy" version of events.
    • Criteria for Selection:
      • Use the following criteria to evaluate each theory (score each from 1-5, with 5 being the best):
        1. Research availability: How easy will it be to find credible sources for both the official story and conspiracy?
        2. Interest level: How curious are you about exploring this theory?
        3. Complexity of competing hypotheses: How many plausible explanations can be found for this theory?
        4. Group or individual work: Consider whether this project would be better suited to working in a group or individually. Reflect on how collaboration (or working alone) might enhance your ability to critically analyze the theory.
      • Develop two additional criteria that are important to you in selecting your theory and explain why these are significant to your project.
    • Weighing Options:
      • Based on your scores and the group/individual decision, identify your top 2 theories. For each, list the pros and cons of investigating it for your capstone project.

    Rubric for Stage 2: Consideration and Discretion

    Criterion 1: Range of Conspiracy Theories Explored

    Meets Expectations

    • Content: The student identifies five or more conspiracy theories and provides a brief summary of both the official story and the conspiracy theory for each.
    • Variety: The theories span a wide range of topics, showing that the student has explored multiple areas of interest (e.g., government cover-ups, historical events, science-related conspiracies).
    • Detail: The summaries are clear, precise, and concise.

    Approaches Meeting Expectations

    • Content: The student identifies three to four conspiracy theories and provides brief summaries for both the official story and the conspiracy theory.
    • Variety: The theories may come from a narrower range of topics or show limited exploration.
    • Detail: Summaries are somewhat vague or lack clear distinction between the official story and the conspiracy.

    Fails to Meet Expectations

    • Content: The student identifies fewer than three theories, or the summaries are incomplete.
    • Variety: The student does not explore a variety of topics, and the theories listed are overly narrow or repetitive.
    • Detail: Summaries are unclear, missing, or irrelevant.

    Criterion 2: Application of Provided Criteria

    Meets Expectations

    • Content: The student evaluates all five theories based on the four provided criteria (research availability, interest level, complexity, and group/individual decision) and assigns a score from 1-5 for each criterion.
    • Explanation: Provides a clear and logical explanation for each score, showing thoughtful consideration of how the criteria apply to each theory.
    • Depth: The analysis reflects the student’s deep engagement with the material and critical thinking about the merits and drawbacks of each theory.

    Approaches Meeting Expectations

    • Content: The student evaluates three to four theories using the provided criteria.
    • Explanation: Provides some explanation for each score, but the reasoning may be shallow or incomplete.
    • Depth: The analysis shows limited critical thinking or engagement with the material.

    Fails to Meet Expectations

    • Content: The student evaluates fewer than three theories, or the evaluations are incomplete or irrelevant.
    • Explanation: Provides little to no explanation for the scores assigned, or the explanations are unclear or off-topic.
    • Depth: The analysis lacks depth or is missing altogether.

    Criterion 3: Development of Additional Criteria

    Meets Expectations

    • Content: The student develops two additional criteria and explains why these criteria are important for selecting a theory.
    • Relevance: The additional criteria are relevant to the project and reflect thoughtful consideration of what will make a successful capstone project.
    • Explanation: Provides a clear, precise, and concise explanation for how each criterion applies to their decision-making process.

    Approaches Meeting Expectations

    • Content: The student develops one additional criterion or develops two but with a lack of depth.
    • Relevance: The additional criterion is somewhat relevant but may not show strong connection to the project.
    • Explanation: Provides a limited explanation or lacks clarity in justifying the additional criterion.

    Fails to Meet Expectations

    • Content: The student does not develop additional criteria or develops irrelevant or unclear criteria.
    • Relevance: The criteria do not contribute meaningfully to the project selection process.
    • Explanation: Provides no explanation or the explanation is unclear or irrelevant.

    Criterion 4: Group or Individual Work Decision

    Meets Expectations

    • Content: The student clearly articulates whether the project is better suited for group or individual work, based on thoughtful consideration of the theory’s complexity, research needs, and their own strengths/weaknesses.
    • Explanation: Provides a detailed explanation that weighs the pros and cons of working in a group vs. individually for each theory, considering how collaboration might affect the depth of analysis.
    • Relevance: The decision is relevant to the project’s scope and reflects a realistic understanding of the benefits and challenges of group vs. individual work.

    Approaches Meeting Expectations

    • Content: The student provides a decision about group or individual work but with less depth or explanation.
    • Explanation: Provides some reasoning, but it may not fully consider how the work will be affected by the choice.
    • Relevance: The decision may be somewhat relevant but lacks full engagement with the project’s scope.

    Fails to Meet Expectations

    • Content: The student does not make a clear decision about group or individual work, or the decision is irrelevant to the project.
    • Explanation: Provides no explanation, or the explanation is unclear or irrelevant.
    • Relevance: The decision does not reflect realistic consideration of the project’s scope or the student’s strengths.

    Criterion 5: Weighing Pros and Cons of Top 2 Theories

    Meets Expectations

    • Content: The student identifies their top two conspiracy theories and lists at least three pros and three cons for each.
    • Explanation: Each pro and con is clearly explained, reflecting thoughtful consideration of the theory’s merits and challenges.
    • Balance: The pros and cons show a balanced approach, weighing both the positive and negative aspects of each theory objectively.

    Approaches Meeting Expectations

    • Content: The student identifies two theories but lists fewer than three pros and cons for each, or the pros and cons are less detailed.
    • Explanation: Provides some explanation but may lack depth or balance in considering both positive and negative aspects.
    • Balance: The analysis may lean heavily toward pros or cons, showing less objectivity.

    Fails to Meet Expectations

    • Content: The student identifies fewer than two theories or does not list meaningful pros and cons for each.
    • Explanation: Provides little to no explanation, or the explanation is unclear or irrelevant.
    • Balance: The pros and cons are unbalanced, or the student fails to provide both positive and negative considerations.

    Scoring Guidelines

    • To "Meet Expectations" for this stage, the student must achieve "Meets Expectations" in all five criteria.

    • To "Approach Meeting Expectations," the student achieves "Approaches Meeting Expectations" in at least three criteria and no "Fails to Meet Expectations" in any criterion.

    • "Fails to Meet Expectations" applies if the student receives "Fails to Meet Expectations" in one or more criteria.

    Stage 3: Decision (Making the Final Choice)

    Goal: Make a final decision about which conspiracy theory to research.

    • Questions to ask:
      • Based on your analysis, why did you choose this theory?
      • What resources will you need to complete your research?
      • What challenges might you face when trying to investigate this theory?

    • Example Prompts:

      • Write a decision statement synthesizing your analysis into a clear choice.
      • Explain why you are choosing this conspiracy theory over the others, taking into account:
        • Your interest
        • The complexity of the competing hypotheses

        • Availability of evidence for and against the theory

      “I chose [conspiracy theory] because it has multiple explanations and I am most interested in [add explanations], is relevant because [add relevance], and I am most interested in exploring the evidence that contradicts the [official story].”

    Rubric for Final Decision: Conspiracy Theory Selection

    Meets Expectations

    • Decision Statement: The student provides a clear and well-reasoned decision statement, identifying the conspiracy theory they will research.
    • Interest: The student explains why they are personally interested in the theory and how that interest will help sustain their engagement.
    • Complexity: The student clearly explains the complexity of competing hypotheses, identifying at least two plausible explanations (official story vs. conspiracy).
    • Evidence Availability: The student provides a thoughtful explanation of the availability of evidence for and against the theory, showing understanding of potential research challenges.
    • Challenges: The student identifies at least two challenges they might face and explains how they plan to overcome them.

    Example Response (Meets Expectations): "I chose the JFK assassination conspiracy theory because it has multiple competing explanations, including the official story of a lone gunman and conspiracy theories involving organized groups. I am very interested in how this event shaped public trust in the government, and it’s still relevant today due to ongoing debates. There is ample evidence available from both primary sources and expert analyses, but the challenge will be sorting through the large volume of material and identifying credible sources."

    Approaches Meeting Expectations

    • Decision Statement: The student provides a decision statement that identifies their choice, but the reasoning may lack depth or detail.
    • Interest: The student mentions their interest in the theory but does not fully explain how it will sustain their engagement.
    • Complexity: The student mentions the existence of competing hypotheses but may only briefly describe them or miss important details.
    • Evidence Availability: The student identifies some available evidence but does not fully explain how it supports their research or fails to address potential challenges.
    • Challenges: The student mentions one challenge they might face but does not fully explain how they plan to address it.

    Example Response (Approaches Meeting Expectations): "I chose the Moon Landing Hoax theory because it has both the official NASA explanation and conspiracy theories that claim the landing was faked. I find space exploration interesting, and there are a lot of sources on both sides. However, I’m not sure how to find unbiased sources, which might be challenging."

    Fails to Meet Expectations

    • Decision Statement: The student either does not provide a decision statement or provides one that is unclear or irrelevant to the assignment.
    • Interest: The student does not mention their personal interest or provides an unclear or irrelevant explanation.
    • Complexity: The student fails to mention competing hypotheses or provides an unclear or incorrect explanation of the theories.
    • Evidence Availability: The student does not mention the availability of evidence or gives an incomplete or irrelevant response.
    • Challenges: The student does not identify any challenges or provides irrelevant or unclear challenges without explanation.

    Example Response (Fails to Meet Expectations): "I chose the Flat Earth theory because it’s interesting. I’m not sure what the official story is or what evidence is out there, but I think it will be fun to research."

    Scoring Breakdown:

    • Meets Expectations: The student must provide a clear decision statement, address interest, complexity, evidence availability, and identify challenges in detail.
    • Approaches Meeting Expectations: The student provides a decision statement and addresses most components (interest, complexity, evidence, challenges) but with limited depth.
    • Fails to Meet Expectations: The student fails to address key components of the decision, provides unclear reasoning, or does not consider challenges effectively.

    Stage 4: Evaluation (Analyzing Bias and Judgments)

    Goal: Reflect on the decision-making process and evaluate any biases.

    • Questions to ask:
      • Were there any biases in how you approached this decision? Did you have a preference going in?
      • Did you consider the strongest counterarguments to the theory you chose?
      • How could your personal interests or preconceptions influence your analysis?

    • Example Prompts:

      • Reflect on your decision-making process. Identify any biases or assumptions you had going in.
      • Explain how your final choice may have been influenced by your initial interest in the conspiracy theory.
      • In a statement, describe why you feel confident in your choice.

      “I feel confident in choosing [conspiracy theory] because I carefully weighed the evidence for and against it and found [most compelling argument]. I also considered my own biases, like [specific bias that stood out], and made sure they didn’t overly influence my final decision.”

    Rubric for Reflecting on Decision-Making and Evaluating Biases

    Meets Expectations

    • Bias Reflection: The student clearly identifies at least one specific bias or assumption they had going into the decision-making process.
    • Counterarguments: The student explains how they considered the strongest counterarguments to their chosen theory and how these were weighed against their final choice.
    • Influence of Personal Interest: The student discusses how their personal interests or preconceptions might have influenced their analysis and explains how they worked to mitigate those influences.
    • Confidence in Decision: The student provides a clear statement of confidence in their choice, supported by specific reasoning (e.g., weighing evidence, addressing biases).

    Example Response (Meets Expectations): "I feel confident in choosing the JFK assassination conspiracy theory because I carefully considered both the official explanation and the conspiracy theories involving multiple shooters. I realized that my initial interest in government cover-ups might have biased me toward conspiracy theories, so I made sure to weigh credible evidence for both sides. The most compelling argument for me was the amount of conflicting eyewitness testimony, but I worked to ensure that my bias toward distrusting the government didn’t overly influence my conclusion."

    Approaches Meeting Expectations

    • Bias Reflection: The student identifies one bias or assumption but provides limited or general detail about how it affected their decision-making.
    • Counterarguments: The student briefly mentions that they considered counterarguments but may not fully explain how these influenced their final choice.
    • Influence of Personal Interest: The student acknowledges that their personal interest or preconceptions influenced their analysis but does not fully explain how they managed these influences.
    • Confidence in Decision: The student provides a statement of confidence, but the reasoning may be somewhat superficial or not fully supported by evidence.

    Example Response (Approaches Meeting Expectations): "I chose the Moon Landing Hoax theory because I was interested in the idea of space conspiracies. I know this interest influenced me at first, but I tried to look at evidence for both sides. I still think the conspiracy theory is interesting, though it was hard to find solid evidence against the official story."

    Fails to Meet Expectations

    • Bias Reflection: The student fails to identify any biases or assumptions in their decision-making process or provides an unclear or irrelevant reflection.
    • Counterarguments: The student does not mention considering counterarguments or does so in an incomplete or irrelevant way.
    • Influence of Personal Interest: The student does not acknowledge any personal interests or preconceptions or does not explain how these may have influenced their decision.
    • Confidence in Decision: The student provides little to no reflection on why they are confident in their final choice, or the reasoning is unclear or unsupported.

    Example Response (Fails to Meet Expectations): "I chose the Flat Earth theory because I’ve always thought it was cool to think about. I didn’t really have any biases, and I’m confident in my choice because it seems interesting."

    Scoring Breakdown:

    • Meets Expectations: The student must clearly identify biases, consider counterarguments, acknowledge personal influences, and provide a well-reasoned statement of confidence in their choice.
    • Approaches Meeting Expectations: The student addresses most components but with limited detail or depth.
    • Fails to Meet Expectations: The student fails to reflect meaningfully on biases, counterarguments, personal influence, or their confidence in their choice.

    Episode 1: The Decision (decision-making process)

    Feel free to unapologetically rip off any provided scripts. This is not a class on podcasting, it is a class on structured analysis and critical thinking. You are creating a narrative that explains your journey and reports your finding. Episode 1 is dedicated to how you decided to make the decision you made and reflect on the process before you get into it.
